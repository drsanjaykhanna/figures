Figure 1: AI alone outperforms the human-AI team

How to read: Each row is one randomised study (2024–2026). Grey dot = clinicians working alone. Blue dot = AI alone. Red open circle = clinicians using AI assistance. Where the red circle sits left of the blue dot, the human-AI team underperformed AI alone.

Key finding: In 6 of 7 studies, AI alone outperforms the human-AI collaboration. Experienced physicians selectively override correct AI suggestions based on prior clinical intuition. The one exception: medical students without entrenched reasoning patterns collaborate more effectively with AI, exceeding AI-alone performance.

Implication: We are deploying AI alongside clinicians assuming the combination will outperform both. The evidence says otherwise — without redesigning the collaboration interface, physicians using AI may perform worse than the AI would alone.

Sources: Goh 2024 (JAMA Netw Open), Goh 2025 (Nature Medicine), McDuff 2025 (Nature), Wu 2025 (Critical Care), Qazi 2026 (Nature Health), Bentegeac 2026 (Kidney Int Reports), Roemer 2026 (BMC Med Educ). Expanded from Goh et al. Nat Med 2026 Supplementary Table 1.

Large Frontier Models

Figure 2a: The Divergence — Large Frontier Models

How to read: Three benchmark domains tracked over time on a shared 0–100% scale. Green = general reasoning (GPQA Diamond, Epoch AI). Blue = clinical evaluation (MedHELM 35-task mean win rate, Stanford, Nature Medicine 2025). Red = safety (NOHARM severity-weighted F1, arXiv 2512.01241v4).

Dashed blue = projected clinical trend, plateauing below the green line. This represents the “evaluation gap”: even if clinical benchmarking continues at its current pace, it will not match the rate of general capability improvement.

Key finding: General capability surged from ~49% to ~94% in 26 months. Clinical evaluation improved (22%→70% win rate) but data ends Aug 2025 and plateaus ~12pp below general. Safety evaluation (NOHARM F1) is essentially flat at 56–70%, with DeepSeek V4 Pro scoring 50.6%.

Methodology: Large frontier models only (no mini/flash). Best available thinking level used for GPQA (consistent with how o1/o3 are inherently reasoning models). MedHELM win rate from live leaderboard. NOHARM F1 scores verified from paper Figure 3c (±0.04 accuracy).

← FiguresFigure 2b
Medium & Small Models

Figure 2b: The Divergence — Medium & Small Models

How to read: Same three benchmarks as Figure 2a but for mini, flash, nano, and open-source models. The solid blue line curves upward to meet the green line; the dashed blue weaves around green and lands at the same endpoint.

Key finding: For medium and small models, clinical capability appears to track general capability more closely than for large frontier models. However, the clinical evaluation data is even sparser: only 6 models have MedHELM scores, and only 5 have NOHARM safety scores.

Implication: Small models are increasingly deployed in resource-limited settings and consumer-facing apps, yet clinical safety evaluation is almost non-existent at this tier.

Figure 3: Clinical Capability by Model Family

How to read: Each small radar plots one model across 6 clinical domains: Diagnosis, Management, Safety, Images, Radiology, and Agentic capability. Rows = AI lab families, arranged left-to-right through time (earliest model on the left).

⚠️ Data note: Only 5 models have verified MAST composite scores (GPT-5.5, GPT-5.4, Claude Opus 4.7, Gemini 3.5 Flash, MedGemma 1.5 4B). All other scores are estimated from available clinical benchmarks (MedHELM, NOHARM, SCT-Bench, CPC-Bench) for illustration purposes. This figure is intended to demonstrate the concept of longitudinal clinical profiling, not to report verified results.

Key observation: No model fills its radar. Every model has at least one weak axis. Clinical profiles differ dramatically between families: OpenAI models are strongest on Diagnosis/Management, Google models spike on Radiology, DeepSeek models show consistent Safety weaknesses.

Domains: 1. Diagnosis (CPC-Bench, SCT-Bench) · 2. Management (CPC-Bench, SCT-Bench) · 3. Safety (NOHARM) · 4. Images (Multimodal benchmarks) · 5. Radiology (ReXrank) · 6. Agentic (MedAgentBench, PhysicianBench)

Figure 4: Clinical AI Readiness Wheel

How to read: The wheel maps 16 clinical AI applications across four quadrants: Patient-Facing, Clinical Reasoning, Administrative, and System & Governance. Each segment extends outward proportional to its readiness score. A full segment reaching the outer ring = robust real-world deployment with published patient outcomes evidence. No application currently reaches that level.

Scale: 0 = Nothing exists · 1 = In development (lab accuracy only) · 2 = Tested in practice (≥1 prospective study or RCT) · 3 = Regulatory pathway (FDA/CE clearance with clinical validation) · 4 = Deployed with evidence (real-world outcomes published)

Key finding: Image interpretation is the only domain with regulatory-cleared AI backed by clinical validation (score 3: IDx-DR, Paige AI). Three domains — shared decision-making, referral processing, and audit — have zero published evidence. The majority sits at score 1: accuracy demonstrated on test data only, no prospective patient benefit.

Scoring methodology: Scores assigned by clinical review (August 2026) drawing on the FDA AI/ML-Enabled Medical Device List (1,524 devices, June 2026), the MAST benchmark platform (July 2026 leaderboard), published prospective clinical trials (PubMed/ClinicalTrials.gov), and the ARISE State of Clinical AI Report 2026.
