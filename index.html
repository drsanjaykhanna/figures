AI vs Human Performance

Left panel (text-based): Each row is one randomised study (2024 to 2026). Grey dot: clinicians alone. Blue dot: AI alone. Red open circle: clinicians using AI.

In 6 of 7 studies, AI alone outperformed the human-AI team on text-based clinical tasks. Experienced physicians selectively overrode correct AI suggestions based on prior clinical intuition. The exception: medical students without entrenched reasoning collaborated more effectively with AI.

Right panel (multimodal): Data pending. Anastasia Perez Ternent and colleagues at ARISE have completed a systematic review of studies with human-AI arms across imaging and multimodal tasks, where the pattern may differ. In imaging studies, human-AI collaboration has shown diagnostic improvement, suggesting the text-based finding does not generalise across modalities.

Sources: Goh 2024 (JAMA Netw Open), Goh 2025 (Nature Medicine), McDuff 2025 (Nature), Wu 2025 (Critical Care), Qazi 2026 (Nature Health), Bentegeac 2026 (Kidney Int Reports), Roemer 2026 (BMC Med Educ).

The Divergence

Three domains tracked over time for 40 foundation models (May 2024 to July 2026). General reasoning capability is surging. Clinical task performance lags behind. Safety benchmark performance lags further still.

Green line: General Capability. Epoch AI GPQA Diamond scores for 32 models. Trend line only; different model set from the clinical and safety dots.

Red dots: Safety Eval. DoNoHarm v2 Balanced Accuracy: mean recall across all harm severity categories, treating rare severe harms equally with common benign ones. Same 40 models as the blue dots. A model scoring 30% correctly classifies harm severity less than a third of the time.

← FiguresThe Detection Gap

The Detection Gap

Left panel: Two benchmark harm rates from DoNoHarm v2. Blue: severe harm rate. Amber: omission rate (100 minus Recall). Both are improving. 39 models from MAST.

Right panel: If these models were deployed at scale, would existing post-market surveillance detect a safety signal? Monte Carlo simulation (10,000 iterations). The dashed connector between panels links the benchmark rates to the detection question.

Blue curve (commission): wrong advice acted on. The harm is visible. A patient deteriorates and the doctor traces it back. Detection reaches 94% by year 4.

Amber curve (omission): a referral not suggested, a test not ordered. Nobody connects the outcome to the AI. Detection: 0.6% at year 4.

Why the gap exists. Both curves use the same harm rate (0.1%), the same reporting system, and the same deployment scale. The only difference is attribution. When AI gives wrong advice, someone notices (P = 80%) and attributes it to the tool (P = 40%). When AI omits a recommendation, the chance of noticing drops to 30% and attribution to 2%. These probabilities are drawn from Beta distributions across 10,000 iterations using standard pharmacovigilance signal detection rules (PRR and BCPNN).

The cerivastatin comparator. A statin recalled at year 4 for causing rhabdomyolysis at a 0.1% rate. The same spontaneous reporting system that caught a dangerous drug in 4 years would need over 25 years to detect an AI omission at the same harm rate, and would succeed only 3.8% of the time.

80%+ of severe AI errors are omissions (NOHARM, Wu et al. 2026). The dominant harm type is the undetectable one.

Source: Khanna et al. (in preparation). Detection threshold calibrated against Sprint Fidelis ICD lead recall (575 reports/year).

← FiguresClinical AI Readiness

Clinical AI Readiness

16 clinical AI applications scored on a 0 to 5 readiness scale. Each segment extends outward proportional to how far that application has progressed toward safe, evidence-based deployment.

The scale: 0 = no evidence. 1 = retrospective studies only. 2 = at least one prospective study. 3 = pre-regulatory pathway (CE mark, MHRA Airlock, breakthrough designation) or deployed commercially without regulation. 4 = FDA cleared (510(k) or De Novo). 5 = deployed at scale with published post-market outcomes data.

The amber segments. Five applications sit at rung 3 in amber: deployed at scale with no regulatory pathway. These are: triage (Epic, Babylon), differential diagnosis (Glass Health, OpenEvidence, Doximity Ask), prescribing (Epic CDS alerts at 85% of Epic customers), documentation (DAX Copilot serving approximately 600,000 clinicians, Abridge at 300+ health systems, Nabla at 85,000+ clinicians), and clinical coding (Epic Penny, 3M AI). None have FDA clearance. None are required to report adverse events.

The single green spike. Image interpretation is the only application reaching rung 5: 1,218 FDA-cleared devices, deployment at scale (Aidoc, Viz.ai), and post-market outcomes evidence (MASAI trial, 100,000+ women).

The purple segment. Symptom checking has CE marking in Europe (Ada Health, Babylon) but no FDA clearance.

The empty segments. Shared decision-making and audit have no meaningful published evidence or commercial product.

Sources: Evidence levels from publication corpus (3,086 papers tagged by clinical task and evidence level). FDA clearance from the FDA AI/ML-Enabled Medical Device List (1,524 devices, June 2026). Deployment data from vendor announcements and published implementation studies. Pre-regulatory status from CE mark registrations, MHRA AI Airlock Phase 2 report (July 2026), and FDA breakthrough device designations.

← FiguresCleared but Unmonitored

FDA AI-Enabled Medical Device List by decision year × openFDA MAUDE report counts for each majority-AI product code · 2026 is a part year

Authorisation accelerates. The record stays empty.

The bars along the top. FDA authorisations of AI-enabled devices by year. 18 in 2016. 333 in 2025. 2026 is a part year and should not be read as a decline.

The strip below. Each of the 66 majority-AI product codes, ranked by how many adverse event reports it has ever received.

What the shape shows

49 of the 66 codes have never received a single report. Not one, in the entire history of the database.

Two cases deserve stating on their own.

QIH is the main code for automated radiological image processing. 274 AI devices sit on it. Between them they have produced 10 reports. Reading the narratives, 6 describe the same patient-record merge defect at one manufacturer, filed separately over eighteen months.

PIB is the code for autonomous diabetic retinopathy screening. This is AI that makes a diagnosis with no clinician reviewing it, first cleared in 2018 and the first of its kind ever authorised anywhere. Seven such devices are now marketed. In eight years the code has received one report, and it was filed by a bystander who watched the software miss glaucoma and report only that diabetic retinopathy was absent.

How to read this responsibly

Two explanations fit equally well. Either these devices are adjunctive, a clinician checks every output, and the safety net genuinely works. Or a wrong output that a clinician acts on is recorded as a clinical outcome and never reaches the regulator at all.

Nothing in this data separates those two. Both would produce an empty database. That is the whole problem in one sentence, and it is why the answer to “is it safe to deploy at scale” cannot currently be evidence-based in either direction.

Also true. Devices cleared recently have had less time to accumulate reports, so part of the emptiness is simply youth. That does not explain PIB, where devices have been deployed for eight years, nor QIH, where 274 products have produced ten reports. Recalls behave the same way: 25 across 11 of the 66 codes, against 860 for a single conventional CT code.

← FiguresTrials vs Results

ClinicalTrials.gov API v2, queried August 2026 · terms: “large language model” OR LLM OR “generative AI” OR ChatGPT OR “foundation model” · publications identified by NCT-number linkage, not by literature search

The trials are running. The results are not appearing.

Figure 1 showed that regulatory surveillance for generative AI does not exist. This asks the obvious follow-up. If not there, then where? Clinical trials are the other place safety evidence is supposed to come from.

Counting results properly, not just results posted

The obvious version of this figure counts how many trials posted results to the register. That number is 5, and on its own it is misleading, because a trial can publish a full paper in a journal and never return to the register at all.

So both routes are counted here. A trial is treated as having reported if either it posted results on the register, or a publication exists that is linked to its registration number. That linkage is automatic: when a paper cites its NCT number, PubMed records it and the register attaches it to the study. No literature search is involved.

How the figure is built, and why it is fair

Each bar is the set of trials that completed in that year. The solid portion inside the same bar is how many of those have since reported. Numerator and denominator are the same group, so the percentage cannot be inflated by mixing cohorts.

Completion year is the right clock, not registration year. A trial registered in 2025 has mostly not finished, so grouping by registration would fill the recent bars with trials that could not possibly have reported yet. Grouping by completion starts counting only once reporting becomes possible.

Only the 2026 bar is marked too recent. A trial that finished this year has not had time to write up and publish.

What the mature cohorts say

Trials completing in 2023, 2024 and 2025 report at 40%, 43% and 31%. Across everything that completed between 2020 and 2025, 39 of 108 have reported.

So roughly two trials in three produce nothing anyone can find. That is stable across cohorts and is not an artefact of recency.

Why registration matters here

Registering a trial before it starts is what prevents selective reporting. It creates a public record of what was planned, so a study producing an unwelcome answer cannot quietly disappear. It is how we came to know about unpublished drug trials.

Across all 148 completed trials, 5 posted results on the register and 42 have findings traceable by either route, leaving 106 with nothing. The gap between 5 and 42 matters: 37 completed trials did publish, just not where the register would show it. Counting only the register would have understated reporting eightfold.

The growth is the urgent part

2 trials first posted in 2021. 8 in 2022. 20 in 2023. 54 in 2024. 160 in 2025. 195 already in 2026 with months still to run.

Activity is scaling roughly threefold a year while the published evidence base stays near nothing. The gap is widening, not closing.

The limitation of this method, stated plainly. A publication is only found if it cites the trial’s registration number or the sponsor linked it manually. Papers reporting a registered trial without naming the registration are invisible to this approach, and that is known to be a substantial minority. So 42 is a floor and 106 is a ceiling, and the true reporting rate sits somewhere above 28%. This is automated record linkage, not a systematic review, and it should never be described as one. What it does establish is a lower bound on the problem using a method that can be re-run in minutes rather than months.

← FiguresResearch vs Safety

PubMed E-utilities by publication year · clinical AI against the subset also indexed for safety, harm, error or adverse events · 2026 excluded as a part year

The field grows faster than the study of its risks

The bars. Papers on clinical AI. 2,436 in 2020, 17,977 in 2025. Sevenfold in five years.

The line. The share of those papers also indexed under safety, harm, error or adverse events. 5.3% in 2020, 8.5% in 2025.

What this does and does not say

Safety research is growing faster than the field overall, from 129 papers to 1,534. That is a real improvement and should be stated plainly rather than buried.

But more than nine papers in ten are still about what these systems can do rather than how they fail. And almost none of the safety literature is post-deployment. It is benchmark evaluation, bias auditing and prospective validation, all necessary, none of which tells you what happened after the system was switched on in a hospital.

Why this figure is included

Because counting research volume is a crude proxy for a field’s maturity, and it becomes influential precisely because it is simple. The Stanford HAI index uses it. Applying the same crude proxy to safety shows the imbalance more directly than any argument would.

Taken with the rest: the regulator records almost nothing, the trial register returns almost nothing, and the literature is overwhelmingly about capability. There is no source in which the safety evidence is quietly hiding.

Method limits. This uses keyword and indexing terms, so it will miss safety work that does not use those words and will catch papers mentioning safety only in passing. Absolute counts should be treated as approximate. The trend and the ratio are robust to reasonable changes in the search terms. 2026 is a part year and is excluded from the chart.

FDA AI-Enabled Device List (through March 2026) · Systematic review of ClinicalTrials.gov + WHO ICTRP, August 2026 (178 GenAI oncology trials) · Market revenue: Precedence Research, MarketsandMarkets (2024–25 est.) · Sources shown at equal visual weight to compare distribution

Where the field bets — and how ready those bets are

Left panel. Three signals of revealed preference — where clinical AI investment actually flows. Each signal is normalised to equal visual weight so the chart compares distribution within each signal, not magnitude between them.

The three signals

Regulatory clearance (blue): 1,524 FDA AI/ML-enabled device clearances. 82% are diagnostic imaging tools. Cardiovascular and neurology account for 17%. Treatment planning, patient engagement, and documentation have near-zero regulatory presence.

Market size (green): Sector revenue estimates. Clinical decision support ($3.95B) is the largest sector, followed by diagnostic imaging ($1.65B), patient engagement ($1.49B), and clinical documentation ($1.45B). Screening estimated at ~$0.6B.

Clinical trial investment (orange): 178 registered clinical trials testing generative AI or large language models in oncology. Diagnosis accounts for 37%, treatment planning 23%, patient engagement 19%.

Right panel — evidence readiness

For each of the five use cases, four binary trial-quality indicators are computed across all trials. Readiness is the mean of the four percentages.

Real patients: % using real patient data. Comparator: % with a control group or benchmark. Safety: % with any safety endpoint. Outcomes: % measuring any patient outcome.

The highest readiness is Clinical Decision Support at 43%. The lowest is Screening at 22%. No use case exceeds 45%.

Key findings

Regulatory and R&D are investing in different places. FDA clearances concentrate in diagnostic imaging. GenAI trials are diversifying into treatment planning, patient engagement, and screening — domains with near-zero regulatory presence.

Market investment does not predict evidence quality. Clinical decision support has the largest market ($3.95B) and the highest readiness (43%), but still only 32% of trials measure safety.

Scope differences. FDA and market data cover all clinical AI. The trial data is GenAI in oncology specifically. Market figures are revenue estimates, not VC investment; they are the best available proxy for where private capital flows by use case. Screening market size estimated from adjacent segments.

← FiguresAugmentation, Not Automation

Anthropic Economic Index v1–v2 (2025), observed Claude API usage classified by O*NET task taxonomy · Healthcare tasks extracted from job_exposure and task-level datasets · Overall AI baseline from same source

AI assists healthcare. It does not replace it.

The Anthropic Economic Index classifies real-world Claude API usage into automation (AI performs the task) and augmentation (AI assists a human performing the task). This figure shows that classification for five healthcare tasks, with the overall AI baseline for comparison.

Patient advisory tasks (treatment advice, prevention counselling) show 0% automation and near-100% augmentation. Even analytical tasks (diagnosis, interpreting histories) are predominantly augmentation. Healthcare is an outlier: overall AI usage is roughly 50/50 automation–augmentation, but healthcare skews almost entirely to augmentation.

The pattern: AI in healthcare currently assists clinicians with information processing. It does not independently make clinical decisions or directly advise patients.

Limits. AEI captures only Claude API usage, not all AI tools. Dedicated clinical AI devices (imaging, ECG interpretation) would not appear. The finding is that general-purpose LLM usage in healthcare is augmentation-dominant — dedicated device-based AI may show different patterns. Tasks are from O*NET taxonomy; real clinical workflows are more granular.
