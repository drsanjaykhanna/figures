Clinical AI Safety

What the surveillance data can and cannot tell us · August 2026

If a government asked today whether it is safe to roll out AI decision support across a health service, an honest answer would have to begin by admitting we have no reliable way of finding out.

That is not an argument from principle. It is what the data shows. Every figure here is built from free public sources and can be regenerated from the code included alongside.

The finding is that the instrument we would rely on cannot see the thing we are worried about. Not that clinical AI is dangerous, and not that it is safe. That we would not currently know either way.

The evidence runs out exactly where the question starts

Start with the question being asked. A government is not really asking whether software that measures a blood vessel on a scan is safe. It is asking about software that reads notes, summarises them, suggests a diagnosis and sorts patients into queues. So the useful thing to measure is how much safety evidence exists for each kind of AI as you move towards that one.

What the three groups are

Imaging and diagnostic devices. Software that analyses a scan, a slide or a trace and returns a measurement or a flag. This is what the FDA has actually cleared. 688 of them sit on product codes where AI devices are the majority of what is present.

Clinical decision support. Software that watches a patient record and tells a clinician something about that patient, such as a sepsis risk score. Almost none of it is cleared as a device. The one substantial example in the data is Cerner's sepsis alerting, and Cerner files its reports while formally stating the product "may not be, by definition, a medical device".

Generative AI and large language models. The technology actually being proposed. The FDA has stated it cannot identify devices containing large language models within its own data. So the report count is not merely low. It is zero, and it is zero because no route exists to file one.

What a product code is, and why everything depends on it

Every device the FDA clears is given a three-letter code describing what it does. When an incident is reported, that code travels with the report. Grouping reports by code is how a regulator notices that a whole class of device is failing.

This only works when the code identifies the technology. It does for a "Radiological Computer-Assisted Triage And Notification Software". It does not for a ventilator that happens to contain a model. That distinction governs every number in these figures and is set out in Figure 2.

What the figure shows

218 adverse event reports for the entire cleared imaging and diagnostic estate, across roughly a decade of deployment. One death. For decision support, 32 reports, all voluntary. For generative AI, nothing at all.

Meanwhile 456 trials of these systems in medicine are registered and 148 have completed. Of those, 106 have produced no findings traceable anywhere, either on the register or in the indexed literature.

The shape is the argument. Surveillance capacity falls away precisely as the technology becomes more autonomous, more general, and more like the thing being proposed for national deployment.

What this cannot tell you. These are counts of reports, not counts of harm. There is no denominator anywhere: we do not know how many times any of these devices was used, so no rate can be calculated. A low count is equally consistent with a safe technology and with a technology whose failures are invisible to the reporting system. Nothing in this data separates those two, and that is the finding rather than a flaw in it.

How 1,524 devices become 149 usable reports

This figure exists because the obvious way to do this analysis is wrong, and getting it wrong produces a confident answer that is false. Each step is a decision, and each is stated here so it can be argued with.

Step 1. Start from the FDA's own list

1,524 devices, published by the FDA as its AI-Enabled Medical Device List. This is not a definition of AI invented for this work. It is theirs.

Step 2. Find which product codes they sit on

172 codes. But codes are shared. A code created for AI triage software contains AI triage software. A code for blood glucose meters contains 509 devices, two of which happen to use machine learning.

Step 3. Keep only codes where AI is the majority

For every code, count the AI devices on it against every device ever cleared on it. If AI devices are at least half, reports against that code are about AI. If they are two out of 509, they are not.

66 codes pass, carrying 688 devices. 836 devices, 55% of the FDA's own AI list, fail and are excluded.

That exclusion is itself a finding. More than half of America's cleared AI devices sit on codes so mixed that their incidents cannot be separated from conventional equipment. They are not under-reported. They are unfindable.

Why this step matters. An earlier version of this analysis skipped it, and instead assumed product codes beginning Q or S were AI codes. They are not. Q simply means recently created. That error pulled in dental drills, shoulder implants and tremor stimulators, and produced an apparent excess of patient injury attributed to AI. The injuries were real. They came from surgical instruments. The finding was an artefact and has been withdrawn.

Step 4. Retrieve every report

747 filings across those 66 codes.

Step 5. Collapse duplicates

A filing is not an event. DermaSensor has 11 filings describing 2 defects, nine of them word for word identical. Across the whole set, 747 filings describe roughly 479 distinct problems.

Step 6. Remove physical products

Two codes scrape past the majority threshold on very small denominators: a knee implantation system (1 AI device of 2) and surgical blood loss estimation (3 of 6). Both are physical products, and between them they hold 529 filings. Removing them leaves 218.

Step 7. Read them

Every remaining narrative was read individually rather than pattern-matched. 149 describe the software producing a wrong clinical answer. 136 of those, 91%, come from one company. That is Figure 3.

Where this is soft. The 50% majority threshold is a judgement, not a standard. The denominator counts 510(k) clearances and so misses devices authorised by other routes, which makes the proportion approximate and causes one code to compute above 100%. Removing the two physical-product codes is defensible but discretionary, and keeping them would change the headline. If this work goes further, those choices should be fixed in writing before anyone looks at the answer.

The safety record measures effort, not risk

The most important thing in this dataset. Of the 218 adverse event reports covering every FDA-cleared AI device that can be identified, 139 come from one product: HeartFlow, which simulates blood flow through the coronary arteries using a CT scan.

That alone would be interesting. What matters is how those reports came to exist at all.

They found their own errors

85% of HeartFlow's reports begin with the company saying it found the problem itself. The recurring wording is "as part of HeartFlow's internal review, we identified a potential false negative". Nobody complained. The company re-analyses cases it has already delivered, finds instances where it missed a narrowed artery, and files them with the regulator.

108 of the 139 use the words false negative explicitly. A false negative here means the software told a cardiologist an artery was fine when it was not.

What it looks like when a clinician notices instead

"The case went for HeartFlow analysis and FFRCT came back clearly negative in the proximal RCA. I am told now that the patient developed unstable angina and underwent cath, showing >90% stenosis in the proximal RCA."

That is one report. There are seven of that kind. The other 129 are from the company's own quality process (118 self-identified, 11 flagged by both company review and a customer, 3 unclear).

Why this reframes everything

Remove HeartFlow and 687 other cleared AI devices have produced 79 reports between them. Not because those devices are better. Because nobody is checking.

So the count of AI errors in FDA data is not a measure of how often AI is wrong. It is a measure of who volunteers to look. A company with a rigorous internal audit appears, in the public record, to have the most dangerous AI product in America. A company with no audit process appears flawless.

Any policy that rewards a low adverse event count therefore rewards not looking. That is a design flaw in the surveillance system, and it is visible in the data rather than argued from theory.

In fairness to HeartFlow. Nothing here suggests their product is unsafe or that they have done anything wrong. The opposite. They are the only manufacturer in this dataset behaving the way you would want all of them to behave, and the system currently penalises them for it. Their analyses also involve trained human analysts, so some reports cite analyst error rather than model error. Both are counted here as the delivered output being wrong, because that is what reaches the patient.

The categories do not carry meaning

Every MAUDE report is classified as a death, an injury or a malfunction. That single field is what any large-scale analysis counts, including the standard pharmacovigilance methods used throughout drug safety. This figure shows what those labels contain once you read underneath them.

A death recorded as a malfunction

From Cerner's sepsis alerting, on a report about alerts failing to display:

"During the course of this investigation, Cerner was also informed of a patient death. Cerner was not provided the details."

Filed as a malfunction. The death appears in the text and not in the field, so no count of deaths will ever find it.

108 missed arterial narrowings recorded as malfunctions

HeartFlow's false negatives are filed 127 malfunction to 12 injury. A missed 90% narrowing in a coronary artery is, in this system, a malfunction.

One defect counted eleven times

DermaSensor, a skin cancer aid, has 11 filings. Nine are identical text describing a single configuration fault that produced falsely low scores and, in the company's own words, "may impact the clinician's care and result in delay of referral to a dermatologist". Counting filings would treat that as eleven safety events.

Reports filed under codes that have nothing to do with them

NVI is the product code for a k-nearest-neighbour classifier used in autoimmune diagnosis. It has one report. That report is about a neurovascular stent. QJB is a malnutrition monitor code and its one report is a glucose sensor. A code for over-the-counter sleep apnoea AI contains a fitness band that burned somebody's arm.

What follows from this

This is why the disproportionality analysis that originally drove this work has been withdrawn rather than repaired. Those methods, the proportional reporting ratio and the Bayesian information component, compare how often an event type appears for one product against another. They are sound methods and they work well in drug safety. They depend on the event type meaning something. Here it does not.

The honest conclusion is stronger than the statistic would have been. You cannot currently measure clinical AI safety from this data using any method that counts categories, because the categories are unreliable. Reading the narratives is the only approach that works, and it does not scale beyond a few hundred reports.

This is not a criticism of MAUDE's designers. The system was built for physical devices, where malfunction, injury and death are distinct and observable. Software that produces a wrong recommendation does not fit those categories, and the harm that follows is written into the patient's notes as a clinical outcome, never as a device event. This is a structural mismatch rather than negligence. Published work in JAMA Internal Medicine has separately documented severity mis-coding in MAUDE for conventional devices as well.

Authorisation accelerates. The record stays empty.

The bars along the top. FDA authorisations of AI-enabled devices by year. 18 in 2016. 333 in 2025. 2026 is a part year and should not be read as a decline.

The strip below. Each of the 66 majority-AI product codes, ranked by how many adverse event reports it has ever received.

What the shape shows

49 of the 66 codes have never received a single report. Not one, in the entire history of the database.

Two cases deserve stating on their own.

QIH is the main code for automated radiological image processing. 274 AI devices sit on it. Between them they have produced 10 reports. Reading the narratives, 6 describe the same patient-record merge defect at one manufacturer, filed separately over eighteen months.

PIB is the code for autonomous diabetic retinopathy screening. This is AI that makes a diagnosis with no clinician reviewing it, first cleared in 2018 and the first of its kind ever authorised anywhere. Seven such devices are now marketed. In eight years the code has received one report, and it was filed by a bystander who watched the software miss glaucoma and report only that diabetic retinopathy was absent.

How to read this responsibly

Two explanations fit equally well. Either these devices are adjunctive, a clinician checks every output, and the safety net genuinely works. Or a wrong output that a clinician acts on is recorded as a clinical outcome and never reaches the regulator at all.

Nothing in this data separates those two. Both would produce an empty database. That is the whole problem in one sentence, and it is why the answer to "is it safe to deploy at scale" cannot currently be evidence-based in either direction.

Also true. Devices cleared recently have had less time to accumulate reports, so part of the emptiness is simply youth. That does not explain PIB, where devices have been deployed for eight years, nor QIH, where 274 products have produced ten reports. Recalls behave the same way: 25 across 11 of the 66 codes, against 860 for a single conventional CT code.

The trials are running. The results are not appearing.

Figure 1 showed that regulatory surveillance for generative AI does not exist. This asks the obvious follow-up. If not there, then where? Clinical trials are the other place safety evidence is supposed to come from.

Counting results properly, not just results posted

The obvious version of this figure counts how many trials posted results to the register. That number is 5, and on its own it is misleading, because a trial can publish a full paper in a journal and never return to the register at all.

So both routes are counted here. A trial is treated as having reported if either it posted results on the register, or a publication exists that is linked to its registration number. That linkage is automatic: when a paper cites its NCT number, PubMed records it and the register attaches it to the study. No literature search is involved.

How the figure is built, and why it is fair

Each bar is the set of trials that completed in that year. The solid portion inside the same bar is how many of those have since reported. Numerator and denominator are the same group, so the percentage cannot be inflated by mixing cohorts.

Completion year is the right clock, not registration year. A trial registered in 2025 has mostly not finished, so grouping by registration would fill the recent bars with trials that could not possibly have reported yet. Grouping by completion starts counting only once reporting becomes possible.

Only the 2026 bar is marked too recent. A trial that finished this year has not had time to write up and publish.

What the mature cohorts say

Trials completing in 2023, 2024 and 2025 report at 40%, 43% and 31%. Across everything that completed between 2020 and 2025, 39 of 108 have reported.

So roughly two trials in three produce nothing anyone can find. That is stable across cohorts and is not an artefact of recency.

Why registration matters here

Registering a trial before it starts is what prevents selective reporting. It creates a public record of what was planned, so a study producing an unwelcome answer cannot quietly disappear. It is how we came to know about unpublished drug trials.

Across all 148 completed trials, 5 posted results on the register and 42 have findings traceable by either route, leaving 106 with nothing. The gap between 5 and 42 matters: 37 completed trials did publish, just not where the register would show it. Counting only the register would have understated reporting eightfold.

The growth is the urgent part

2 trials first posted in 2021. 8 in 2022. 20 in 2023. 54 in 2024. 160 in 2025. 195 already in 2026 with months still to run.

Activity is scaling roughly threefold a year while the published evidence base stays near nothing. The gap is widening, not closing.

The limitation of this method, stated plainly. A publication is only found if it cites the trial's registration number or the sponsor linked it manually. Papers reporting a registered trial without naming the registration are invisible to this approach, and that is known to be a substantial minority. So 42 is a floor and 106 is a ceiling, and the true reporting rate sits somewhere above 28%. This is automated record linkage, not a systematic review, and it should never be described as one. What it does establish is a lower bound on the problem using a method that can be re-run in minutes rather than months.

Safety claims are inherited, sometimes across decades

How most AI devices reach the market

The 510(k) route does not require a clinical trial. A manufacturer shows its device is substantially equivalent to one already on the market, called the predicate. Clearance rests on that comparison rather than on new evidence of safety or benefit.

The predicate had a predicate of its own. That one had another. Safety assurance is inherited down a chain, and the chain can be long.

What was measured

150 clearance summaries for AI devices were downloaded and parsed. 140 name at least one predicate.

The median chain reaches back 3 years. The longest reaches back 21. 9% rest on a device cleared a decade or more earlier, before modern machine learning existed in any recognisable form.

81% cite at least one predicate that is itself an AI device. 19% cite none, meaning the comparator was conventional software.

Why this matters for the policy question

A device cleared in 2026 may be standing on one cleared in 2005. The 2005 device was not a neural network, and its evidence base says nothing about how a modern model behaves on data it has not seen. Equivalence is a regulatory claim, not a clinical one.

This is the mechanism by which a fast-moving technology enters practice on slow-moving evidence, and it applies to every imaging AI device in Figure 5.

An open discrepancy, stated deliberately. Published work by Bracken and colleagues in Clinical Orthopaedics and Related Research reports 62% of orthopaedic AI devices citing non-AI predicates. This analysis finds 19%. The likely cause is method: this extracts every clearance number appearing in the document, which is more permissive than reading each one by hand, and it covers a different mix of devices. The discrepancy is unresolved. The 19% figure should be treated as provisional, and reconciled before it appears in any formal output.

The field grows faster than the study of its risks

The bars. Papers on clinical AI. 2,436 in 2020, 17,977 in 2025. Sevenfold in five years.

The line. The share of those papers also indexed under safety, harm, error or adverse events. 5.3% in 2020, 8.5% in 2025.

What this does and does not say

Safety research is growing faster than the field overall, from 129 papers to 1,534. That is a real improvement and should be stated plainly rather than buried.

But more than nine papers in ten are still about what these systems can do rather than how they fail. And almost none of the safety literature is post-deployment. It is benchmark evaluation, bias auditing and prospective validation, all necessary, none of which tells you what happened after the system was switched on in a hospital.

Why this figure is included

Because counting research volume is a crude proxy for a field's maturity, and it becomes influential precisely because it is simple. The Stanford HAI index uses it. Applying the same crude proxy to safety shows the imbalance more directly than any argument would.

Taken with the rest: the regulator records almost nothing, the trial register returns almost nothing, and the literature is overwhelmingly about capability. There is no source in which the safety evidence is quietly hiding.

Method limits. This uses keyword and indexing terms, so it will miss safety work that does not use those words and will catch papers mentioning safety only in passing. Absolute counts should be treated as approximate. The trend and the ratio are robust to reasonable changes in the search terms. 2026 is a part year and is excluded from the chart.

Four registries, and none of them records clinical AI harm

If regulatory surveillance is thin, the obvious next question is whether the independent incident registries are catching what the FDA misses. Three exist and are widely cited. This compares what each actually contains.

What each one is

OECD AI Incidents and Hazards Monitor. The most official-sounding of the three. Its own subtitle describes it as an "automated media discourse monitor", in Beta, built by scraping news through Event Registry. It carries a disclaimer that its contents do not represent the views of the OECD or its member countries. 16,995 records, of which 1,057 are tagged healthcare, drugs and biotechnology.

AIAAIC. Manually curated incidents and controversies. 2,255 records, 219 health-sector, 23 involving loss of life.

AI Incident Database. Manually curated, the most academically cited of the three. 1,625 incidents, 162 health-related, 42 mentioning a death.

FDA MAUDE. Mandatory and voluntary adverse event reports for regulated devices. 218 on product codes where AI devices are the majority.

The finding

The three registries hold 1,438 health records between them. Almost none concern a regulated clinical AI device.

In the AI Incident Database, of 162 health incidents, exactly one involves a medical device: a 2015 collection of robotic surgery malfunctions. Sixty are consumer platforms, chatbots and social media. Six are insurers and payers. The rest are algorithms in research, employment and public services.

AIAAIC behaves the same way. Of the 25 entries flagged as device or clinical-system related, most are insurance algorithms, transplant allocation systems and chatbots, none of which is an FDA-regulated device and none of which could ever generate a regulatory report.

The OECD monitor cannot be checked this way at all, because it counts news coverage rather than verified incidents. Its top healthcare result on the day of retrieval was an AI stethoscope misdiagnosing heart conditions in pets.

Why this matters

These registries are not failing. They are doing what they were built to do, which is to document AI harms that surface publicly through journalism and litigation. Consumer chatbots and insurance algorithms surface that way. A radiology model that quietly misses a lesion does not.

So there is no second source. If a clinical AI device harms a patient, the only place it could be recorded is the regulatory system, and Figures 3 to 5 show what that system contains. The registries are watching a different world, and the gap between the two is not covered by anything.

Method and its limits. Health-sector and device classification were done by keyword matching over title, description and named deployer, which is coarse and will misclassify at the margins. The exact expressions are in the included code. The registries also use different inclusion criteria, time windows and definitions of an incident, so their totals are not directly comparable to one another; the comparison that carries weight is within each source, between its health records and the device-related subset of those records. The AI Incident Database was retrieved through its GraphQL API from the site origin, because the published export is served from a host that fails behind TLS-inspecting corporate networks.
